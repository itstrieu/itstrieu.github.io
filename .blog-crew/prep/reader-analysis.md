# Reader Analysis

## Who's reading this

People in their late twenties to late thirties who came to tech sideways. Not CS grads who optimized their LinkedIn at 22. People who studied something else, worked in restaurants or nonprofits or classrooms, and then found themselves teaching themselves Python at midnight wondering if they started too late. Many are neurodivergent or suspect they might be. They are not looking for tutorials. They are looking for someone further down a nonlinear path who is honest about what it actually feels like.

A secondary audience: mid-career generalists in AI-adjacent roles who are watching the field reshape itself and trying to figure out whether their breadth is an asset or a liability. They found this blog through the identity piece or a search about career pivots in tech.

## What they get

- **Welcome post**: A promise. Ambitious but generic. Reads like a blog that hasn't found its voice yet. A reader skims it and decides to check back later.
- **Learning Log**: Validation. The raw honesty about having too many tabs open, about learning a lot and still feeling behind, about needing to write things down just to feel sane. This is the first post where a reader feels recognized.
- **Fluent Python**: Practical credibility. Not a tutorial they'd bookmark, but proof that the author is doing the work, not just talking about it. The vulnerability about failing the technical interview is what makes it land.
- **Sunday Summary 1**: A glimpse of volume and drive. The last paragraph, reframing "I'm not doing enough," is the emotional hook. The bullet list itself is mostly for the author, not the reader.
- **Sunday Summary 2**: Same structure, less payoff. No reflection at the end, so it reads like a to-do list someone else checked off. A reader's eyes glaze.
- **Tennis Ball Bot**: The best build post. Real details, real frustration, real pride. The reader walks away thinking, "She's actually building something hard and being honest about how messy it is."
- **Why I Call Myself an AI Systems Builder**: The centerpiece. A reader who has bounced between careers, who has felt the shame of not having a clean answer to "what do you do," will feel this in their chest. It is the reason someone subscribes.

## Strongest moments

1. *"The shame of being almost-good-enough at a dozen things and not able to point to a single one and say this is mine. It's a specific kind of loneliness, being surrounded by people who know what they are and not having an answer when someone asks you the same question."* -- This is the line that gets shared.

2. *"A book can tell you the pattern is valid. It can't make the pattern stop hurting."* -- On reading Range. Devastating in its simplicity.

3. *"For the first time in my life, I was choosing difficulty, and I loved it."* -- The turn. A reader who has been drifting feels the ground firm up under them when they read this.

4. *"Breadth got me to the door. Depth gave me the foundation. AI gave me the speed to use both at once."* -- The thesis. Clean enough to remember.

5. *"This robot is still a work in progress, but it already represents something bigger to me."* -- Quieter than the others, but honest in a way that makes the technical content feel personal.

## Weakest moments

- The **welcome post** reads like a different blog by a different person. The tone is eager and performative in a way that clashes with everything that comes after. A new reader who lands here first might not continue.
- The **Sunday Summaries** are learning logs that work for the author but offer little to the reader. Summary 2 in particular has no reflection, no narrative, no takeaway. Bullet lists of tools touched do not build connection.
- The **Fluent Python** post sits in a no-man's-land: too basic for experienced developers, too dry for the audience drawn in by the identity writing. The code walkthrough is competent but not distinctive.
- There is a 13-month gap between the Tennis Ball Bot post (April 2025) and the AI Systems Builder post (May 2026). The reader has no idea what happened in between. That gap undermines the "building in public" premise.

## The gap

The blog has two modes: raw career introspection (which is excellent) and technical learning logs (which are mostly for the author). What is missing is the middle. Posts that combine the *how I think about a problem* with the *what I actually built*, the way the Tennis Ball Bot post does but applied to the AI systems work at Meta. The identity piece tells us she designs evaluation systems and builds tools that spread organically. A reader wants to see inside that process. Not proprietary details, but the thinking: how she scoped it, what she got wrong, what surprised her. That is the content no one else can write.

Also missing: anything for the reader who just arrived. There is no "start here" path. The blog goes from an outdated welcome post to dense learning logs to one standout essay with nothing connecting them.

## Trust assessment

Trust is high where the writing is vulnerable. The Learning Log, the Tennis Ball Bot post, and especially the AI Systems Builder piece all build trust through specificity and emotional honesty. She names her failures, her shame, her self-doubt. That is hard to fake.

Trust wobbles in two places. First, the welcome post promises a broad content strategy (ethics, neurodiversity, curated resources) that never materializes. A reader who found that post first would feel misled. Second, the Sunday Summaries list impressive volumes of work but offer no proof or reflection, which can read as performative productivity rather than real learning.

The about page is strong. "No content strategy, just the practice of saying things in public" resets expectations in a way that earns trust back.

## One-line reader persona

A nonlinear career-changer in tech who is tired of feeling behind and looking for proof that breadth, honesty, and late starts can still lead somewhere real.
